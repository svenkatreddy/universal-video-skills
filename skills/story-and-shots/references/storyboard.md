# Storyboarding for AI Video

You don't need drawing skills. A storyboard for AI video is a planning
document, not art — boxes, stick figures, and arrows are fine. Its job is to
catch the problems text hides: geography that doesn't connect, pacing with no
breathing room, five close-ups in a row.

## The minimum useful board

One panel per shot. Each panel has:

- A rough sketch (even a rectangle with "Mara L→R" and an arrow counts)
- Shot number, matching the shot list
- Camera line (framing + movement)
- The beat's one-line story job ("she realizes it's not him")
- Duration

Lay the panels in order and read them left to right. You're looking for:

1. **Geography.** Can you trace where everyone is, panel to panel? If a
   character teleports, the audience feels it even if they can't name it.
2. **Shot-size rhythm.** Wide → medium → close-up → wide breathes. Six
   mediums in a row suffocates. Alternate deliberately.
3. **Screen direction.** Arrows should flow consistently. A flip needs a
   reason (and a note in the shot list).
4. **Pacing.** Mark the quiet panels. A video with no quiet shots feels
   frantic; the empty-corner shot in the example is doing real work.

## AI-specific storyboard notes

- **Mark generation strategy per panel.** Text-to-video, image-to-video, or
  "generate the still first, then animate it." The risky shots get the
  careful strategy.
- **Thumbnail the first frame.** For image-to-video shots, the board panel
  *is* the first-frame spec. Describe or generate that still with the same
  care as the video prompt.
- **Note what each shot must preserve.** Carry the Preserve slot from the
  shot-brief onto the panel. Continuity decisions live where eyes land on
  them.

## When to skip it

Single shots, tests, and experiments don't need boards. The rule: board when
shots have to relate to each other — that's usually at three or more shots,
or any time there's dialogue, pursuit, or a reveal.
